# Chapter 1 - Limbo (draft v0, written c. 2013)

> Original draft, extracted from the PDF. Kept for reference. Not the working version.

Beep! Beep! Beep!

“Back-up a just little more, Ron” – I said through a tiny microphone mounted on my helmet.

“Okay, just tell me when to stop.” - Ron’s rough voice echoed clearly through the speaker in my ear. He was backing the last load of leftover waste, mostly dirt, out of the chemical reactor facility and into a nearby ditch.

“Okay, slow it down now.” – I said. This was my first day at this site, where a new base was being built. Ron and I had come to check on the status of things; mostly to make sure the fuel producing modules and water extracting plants were operational. The waste Ron was backing out belonged to one of the water extracting plants.

“That’s good, Ron. You can stop right there!” – I informed him and watched as he proceeded to dump the load into the ditch. Ron was in his forty’s, he was a mechanic, engineer, field scientist, and above all, good man. He had been a dear friend ever since I stepped foot on this place.

“Alright Peter, I’m coming over. About time to head back. Do me a favor and collect the drone, would ya?” – he said.

“Sounds good.” – I acknowledged, ‘Okaying’ with my right thumb.

I used the digital remote controller on my wrist to command the small flying drone to land near me. We called these drones Aerials. They provided a convenient, portable communications network, amongst other things. Extremely helpful little guys. Okay, I thought, just a little to the left… here we go. I entered the command and the drone began its descent. As it flew over me, I caught a glimpse of the evening sky. It was clear; the sun was setting and more stars materialized with every passing moment. It’s getting late – I thought, checking the info hub on my left wrist for the time. The small screen lit up as I touched it, displaying a cluster of rectangular sections, each of them containing some piece information. Time, temperature, atmospheric pressure, oxygen level were among data points displayed.

The digital-green, time section on the hub read 18:01.

The Aerial touched down a couple feet away from me before long, forming a reddish cloud of dust around its buzzing propellers and the ground on which it landed. I walked towards it, shaking off the cold along the way, and bent down to pick up the little guy. One of its tubular landing skids was submerged deep into the ground and I had to dig it out. I could feel the sharp edges of the stones piercing through my

white gloves as I stuck my hands into the stony/sandy mixture. It reminded me of the harshness of this environment. I dug the Aerial out and walked it towards a big, 8-whiled vehicle, equipped with all kinds of gadgets, tools and instruments for field science and mechanical upkeep. I tucked the drone in one of the compartments of the vehicle, lucked it up and walked towards the passenger side. At this point, Ron was making his way towards me.

“Ready to go, Ron?” – I asked.

“Yeah, I am. It’s been a long day.” – He responded.

“Sure has, man. Let’s get back on the road.” – I added and waited for him to come. I took as second and looked around me. The scene was exceptionally aweing. A great, vast, empty world everywhere I looked. Wavy seas of red sand covered the parameter as far as the eye could see. This hellish setting put thoughts of Dante Alighieri’s struggles through the eight circles of hell in my mind. I presumed that Dante would have felt much the same as I did when he walked about the gloomy, cold grounds of Limbo, with the shadow of Hell blackening the place with deathly hopelessness. I felt as one of the shades of Dante’s Limbo: lost. Yet the more I observed, the more I took it in, the stranger the emotions I felt. Maybe this world was alive, and its spirit prevailed everywhere. It surrounded me, it embraced me and it provided the fire that cooked up these feelings within me. Feelings that resembled loneliness, frightfulness, insignificance, and yet, they were strangely calming, peaceful. The scene reached out with all encompassing, welcoming vibes to grab my hand and make me a part of this lonesome world… to make me one with it, to move forward with it and leave the past behind. I can’t just yet. – I thought as I took a deep breath, feeling the coolness of the oxygenated air pumped into my helmet and down my throat.

“Peter!” – I heard Ron yelling from the driver’s seat of the grey truck, “Are you coming?”

“I’m coming, I’m coming!” – I responded. “Sorry, Ron,” – I said as I buckled in, “I was just thinking about some things.”

“I can see that you were. What’s bothering you?” – He stated and asked.

“Well I’m just thinking about home… I really miss it and everyone.” – I said, as memories of my past crept into my thoughts. I had this deep longing to see my friends and family once again. For things to return to the way they were before.

“I guess I feel like I should have done more, Ron. I was in a position to change certain things. I mean, really change things, but I didn’t. I was too week… uhhh, I am too weak. I feel guilty... and maybe rightly so.” – I said, opening up to Ron.

“All I know is that you’re a good man, Peter. You better believe that. I been around for a while now, met a lot of folk in my day, and I can tell you that you’re one of the good guys.” – he said very genuinely. I appreciated it. But Ron didn’t understand, he didn’t know how deep the rabbit hole really went. No one here, for that matter, knew of the truth I held close to me. No one knew it was the real reason I was here.

I would tell them, I just didn’t know how… I didn’t know how they would react, if they would even believe me. I had to tell them soon, I had to alleviate this burden; a burden that was proving to weigh me down to a state of paralysis… I would tell them somehow, when they were ready to hear.

“Anyways, don’t think so much. Don’t overthink things. Like they say, ‘overthinking is the art of creating problems out of nothing.’ Whatever happened happened.

There’s nothing you can do about it now. Focus on the here and now. We need you, the community needs you, Peter.” – He continued.

“Thanks for that Ron. And you’re absolutely right. ” – I smiled and said, glad to have tagged along with him today. I would make things right, for myself and for everyone who struggled alongside me.

“Ahh, don’t even mention it! We’re all in this together now, Peter. I like to think we’re all one big family.” – Ron said.

“I’m glad you feel that way. I do to.” – I responded, smiling yet again. Ron truly was a good man and good friend. I was happy he shared the same vision as me.

“How much farther till we get back to base?” – I asked.

“Won’t be long now. We’re almost back to some warmth and good food.” – Ron said as the 8-wheeler plowed through the wavy terrain.

“I know, I’m hungry and tired.” – I said.

“Ahhh! I lovveee food! And I tell ya what: Marsha and everyone working the kitchens make some of the best meals. We sacrificed a great deal of things coming here, I’m glad good food isn’t among them.” – He said, as he turned to look at me with his ‘overly round’ face, cheerfully smiling, letting his love for good food lighten up the mood.

“So, besides feeling sad from being so far from home, what do you think of our community here?” – He asked.

“I haven’t been here long, so I can’t really pass judgment. But I like it here, Ron. I see a lot of potential for well-being. The people are great; they are intelligent and open-minded. Like you said, it feels like one big family.” – I responded.

“Exactly! The way I see it, its the people, and the connections we make with them, that make life something truly special – even in a place like this...” – He said. His last statement, although subtle, resonated with me deeply. What’s life if one cannot share it with loved ones?

“How long has it been for you here?” – he asked.

“Umm, thirty one days exactly. I can’t believe it’s already been that long. Time really flies by.” – I said.

“It really does, Peter. And you gotta fly with It.” – He said, with a touch of wisdom in his response.

As Ron promised, it wasn’t long before the view of the home base materialized out there in the horizon. The more ground the truck covered, the more detailed the picture become; Man-made buildings of all types of geometric shapes and sizes came in clear view… The most aweing were the glass domes encompassing many of the buildings. As we made our way to the destination, I saw the individual men and women who made up the community. I saw them, through the glass domes, going about their business, attending their individual needs and those of the community.

Greenhouse farms and gardens seemed to cover most of the base. Because of them, we had grains, vegetables and fruits of all kinds. Alongside the farms, where chicken coops, sheepfolds, and corrals filled with cattle. Next to those were square–ish and cylindrical, two–storied, gray colored habitation centers. Just outside the domes, arrays of chemical tanks could be seen working, each one of them creating and extracting various resources such fuel, water, oxygen, etc. A nuclear plant powered the entire community. Because of it, the base was equipped with the power of electricity and the ability to run the machines that made life here possible.

This was the first and largest base built here. It housed around one thousand people. Naturally, given the nature of the circumstances that brought us here, most of the people that made up the community were scientists, engineers, technicians, and doctors; yet there were also musicians, writers, and artists. The harshness of the place, with all the challenge it presented, united our small numbers. It provided just enough pressure to form a highly effective, cohesive force that greatly enhanced

the quality of life and relationships of and between the members of our humble, yet determined, community. It was inspiring to observe the people live day-to-day.

Watch them go about their lives here. At a granular level, the community drew strength from the passions that motivated people, passions that could be used to build and to live a peaceful, pleasant and satisfying life. A musician played his heart out for the sake of playing his heart out; for the sake of breaking barriers between people and to connect them through music. A scientist sought out to understand various different physical and theoretical phenomena; understanding that could be used to solve various physical problems. A philosopher could ponder about the human condition, understand its nature and help provide more robust ideologies. It was remarkable, never before in human history had a society been so naturally cohesive and happy. All made possible because people were free, free to pursue their passions and benefit everyone because of it. This was The Phobos I, the center of it all. It was our home base, our community, our village, our city and the first human settlement in an alien world: Mars.

Before long, Ron and I reached the southern tip of the base and parked the truck next to all the other vehicles. “Finally here!” – Ron exclaimed as he jumped out of the truck.

“Can you take the field equipment back to Alex?” – Ron asked.

“I can do that.” – I responded.

“Okay, let’s meet up at the Muse. Get Alex to come too, we’ll grab some grub and a drink or something.” – He said, clearly anxious to wind down at The Holy Muse, or as we called it The Muse. It was the only place resembling a bar in Phobos, with the special privilege of serving alcohol. In more ways than one, The Muse was our version of an Old West Saloon.

“Yeah man, sounds good.” – I said. We shook hands and parted ways. He made his way north, past the parked vehicles and towards one of the bigger see-through domes of Phobos I, appropriately named RecCen, which stood for Recreation Center.

It was located about a football field’s length away from where we stood and was home to a conglomeration of recreational buildings such as gymnasiums, food courts, theaters and of course, Ron’s favorite place, The Muse.

“Ron!” I yelled before he was entirely out of sight, “Don’t get drunk without us!”

“No promises, Pete!” – He responded jokingly and walked away.

I grabbed the equipment we borrowed for the trip and walked to the warehouse near our parked 8-wheeler. This building simply read Field Work Equipment and Maintenance (FWEM) or “Fwehm”. I walked in and a familiar face greeted me. This was Alex, the man in charge of FWEM where, as the name indicates, all the equipment for fieldwork and experimentation was kept and maintained safely. Alex was in his 30’s, tall, dark-haired with a skinny build. He was another friend I had come to gain in this place. Like Ron, he was a good man, skilled in the art of mechanics, and always wore a smile on his face.

“Pete! You guys are finally back! How was your day? And what did you do with Ron?” – He greeted.

“Hey man!” – I said, “It was a long day. Ron is probably eating and drinking a beer or two at the Muse.”

“That guy is an alcoholic, man. He’s always there.” – Alex said, jokingly.

“Can’t say that I blame him.” – I responded in kind.

“Yea, yea, did you bring me everything?” – he asked, motioning for the Aerial I held in my right hand and the keys to the truck.

“Yes sir! Here you go.” – I said and handed them to him.

“Wanna go meet Ron? Have a drink?” – I asked him.

“Yeah, sure. Just give me a second. I’ll come.” – He responded.

“Sounds good. I’ll just wait for you here.” – I said. He took the Aerial drone in one hand and walked to a nearby desk. He bent down and grabbed what looked like a towel from a plastic beg. I observed as he wiped the dust and dirt from that droid, smiling the whole time. After, he proceeded to walk the drone to one of the FWEM’s long aisles where various drones and other tools where neatly preserved. This place resembled a labyrinth made of these lanes and open areas. How does Alex find his way around here? How does he maintain this place so neatly? – I wondered, I guess he took pride and care in his work - pride and care towards the upkeep of things. I could tell he was happy doing what he did here.

“You done yet? I need a whiskey neat.” – I asked and stated.

“Yeah, I’m done. I’m coming.” – He responded, coming over my way, “wanna take the buggy?” – He asked motioning to a two-sitter buggy near the exit of FWEM.

“Its up to you.” – I responded.

“Let’s do it!” – he said.

“The Muse is literally five minutes from here, do you really wanna go through the trouble?” – I asked.

“Yea man, let’s take a ride, I’m feeling It.” – He said. Alex style of talking was very distinct to the 21st century, urban slang. I hadn’t heard it since my college days.

That hip-hop way of speaking had finally breached into the scientific community – I thought, about century and about 3- generations later.

“Alright, let’s do it.” – I said.

We got onto the small buggy and were on our way. Darkness had befallen Phobos.

All the lights of the base were turned on. Most of the people were inside their habitations centers by now. All was quiet, besides the constant roaring of the buggy’s engine, which could be heard even through the insulation provided by my helmet.

“Pete.” – Alex suddenly said.

“Huh?” – I responded.

“So, guess who came by FWEM today?” – He asked.

“Hmm, I don’t know. Who came by?” – I responded and asked.

“Dr. Jiangsu and Dr. Taylor.” – He said, “they came to ask me if there was something wrong with our communications systems. I told them that if there was something wrong I wasn’t aware of it. They looked concerned… almost scared So I asked them if something was wrong. They didn’t answer, just asked me to take a look at the CommSat systems.”

“What happened after?” – I asked, interested to find out what happened.

“Well the three of us went over to the central CommSat module so I could check it out. I looked it over, checked every log and every configuration file. Everything was fine, I didn’t see anything wrong with It.” – He said, looking troubled, and continued.

“The systems were completely fine. The satellites were fine. Everything was responding normally, as it should… uhhh” – he let out a sigh, “everything was fine except one issue.”

“Well don’t keep me in suspense man, what was the issue?” – I stated, wondering what this man had to say.

“No noise from the blue planet. Nothing. I heard Dr. Jiangsu say that Earth totally cut communications! It’s like everyone disappeared. What could have happened back home?” – He said and asked. I could see uneasiness in his black eyes. The sort of

restlessness caused by the fear for the unknown. I wish I had the comforting answer he was looking for, but I didn’t. My guess at this point was as good as his.

“I really don’t know.” – I said, “I wish that I did…”

“Peter, you know that you were on the last human convoy that came to Mars, right?

You must know something. I heard rumors that ever since you came, communications from Earth have been… weird, cryptic… almost as if everyone is high on drugs. And now, Earth has been totally silent for who-knows-how-long.

What if they cut the convoys too? That means no more people, no more precious Earth resources. We’d all be done in months. Why would they do this?? I just don’t get it!” – He said, fully panicked. I felt so guilty.

“Like I said, I don’t know Alex. But one thing is for certain, we have to be careful and that means being objective. If the rumors are true, we can’t let it be a breeding ground for fear. We cannot afford fear here. Trust me man, we have the best minds the human race has to offer. We will make it somehow. Even without Earth. Trust me, bud.” – I responded trying to comfort him.

“You’re right!” – He responded, “The only thing to fear is fear itself.”

“Exactly.” – I agreed.

“Thanks for making me feel a little better, Pete.” – He said, appreciatively. No need to thank me, Alex I thought, feeling guilty for everything that happened; at the same time, part believed that it had to happen the way it did.

“Let’s had to the Muse, shall we? Ron is probably getting impatient.” – I said. He acknowledged.

---

We arrived at the Muse soon after and parked the buggy just outside the pressurized dome. We neared a small gate, which led to a tunnel that drove underground, below the outer parameter of the dome, and came up inside the pressurized, habitable area. Alex punched a 6-character code on a light blue, holographic codex on the gate; It swung it open seconds later. He walked through and I followed. These tunnels were a little crammed with white fluorescent lights illuminating the futuristic looking, metallic-grey corridor; not a good place to be if you’re claustrophobic – I thought. Midway through the small tunnel, we reached another doorway. It led to pressurized room where we could free ourselves from the need of a space suit. Can’t wait to take this thing off – I thought. We proceeded through to remove our suits.

“Hello, Alex!” – a women’s sweet voice greeted through the telecom speakers of the pressurization/depressurization room.

“Hey Liz. What’s going on?” – Alex greeted back.

“Who’s that with you?” – She asked.

“Hey Liz, it’s Peter. How you doing?” – I responded to Lindsay. She was a medical doctor. Like her voice, Liz was a pleasant person. She was of Asian descent and in her 30’s.

“Hi, Pete! Good to see ya! Ron came through earlier. He told me you guys were checking up on new base. How was that?”

“It was pretty cool, but I can’t like that I’m tired!” – I said.

“I bet!”

“Well, you guys know what to do! Be still and let me take some readings. Alex, you’re up first.” – she said, indicating that she had to take some basic medical measurements (blood pressure, heart rate, brain signals, etc.) through the sensors provided by our suits. The readings were wirelessly sent across a network provided by one of the Areal drones to a monitor in the medical center where Liz could see them.

“Sounds good.” – Alex said.

“Jesus! Did you guys run here? You’re heart rate is way up!”

“Something like that.” – Alex responded.

“Well be careful. Anyways, you seem fine. Peter you’re next.”

“Ok.” – I said.

After a second, she said, “Hmm. Well you’re good too! Go ahead and take those suits off! I will talk to you guys later. Maybe we can do breakfast tomorrow. Pete, you can tell me all about how Phobos II is coming along. Ya?”

“Yea of course, Liz!” – I responded.

“Cool. Talk to you guys later.”

We proceeded to take our suits off. I glanced at Alex as he took off his helmet. For the first time today, I saw his worries painted clearly on his face… he seemed scared and afraid. They would all be frightened if they found out. And fear, once it grips you with its nasty, monstrous clutches, it won’t let go until it breaks you. I couldn’t allow that to happen. Not in this place. I had to be strong; I had to speak the truth and tell them what happened. It was time now.

We came out from under the tunnel to the inside of the RecCen. It was nice being free from the confines of the suit I was wearing all day. Breathing in the ‘natural’ air felt nice as well. The RecCen was quiet and peaceful just like the rest of Phobos this night. The whole place resembled a public park… and we were taking a walk through it to the Northern parameter of the dome, where the Muse resided. Alex didn’t speak a word all the way there. We finally came to a small doorway inscribed with the words: The Muse. The doorway, yet again, led to an underground structure.

The Muse was one of the first buildings built in Mars, which meant it had to be constructed underground, beneath the crushing weight of the Marian surface. All initial habitation areas in Mars had to be constructed underground, it was the only way to find enough pressure to keep the buildings together. Pressurized domes were erected during the later stages of the Mars colonization.

We walked into the Muse and saw that Ron was already posted by the bar, with a mug of beer in his hand. He saw us and waved.

“How’s it going, man.” – Alex greeted Ron, walking over to where he was standing.

“Ahh!” – Ron let out a sigh of relief- relieved to be drinking his beer. “It’s going good.

Better now that you two are finally here. What took you so long?” – He asked.

“Well Pete insisted on taking the buggy for a ride around the town. So I obliged” – Alex responded jokingly.

“Hey, that was all you man.” – I said.

“Don’t worry, Pete, I know it wasn’t you. I’ve known this guy for quite a while now” – Ron responded. Alex smiled, looking guilty as charged.

“Anyways, what do you wanna drink, Alex?” – I asked.

“Get me a Lager, Carlsberg or Amstel.” – He responded.

“Luke, you heard the man. Get us two tall Carlsbergs.” – I told Luke, the bartender of the Muse.

“You got it, boss.” – He acknowledged. Luke was in his late 20’s, had blue eyes- blond hair; he was an honest young man and had a good character. He was also one of the relatively few people to be born on the Red Planet. Most had come from Earth.

“You guys wanna sit somewhere?” – asked Ron.

“Yeah man.” – Responded Alex, “Just pick the spot.”

Without a second wasted, Ron made his way to a booth on the corner near the bar.

Alex and I followed. In all my days here, we always chose the same spot. What is it

that makes us always pick that corner? I wondered. Maybe we just wanted to dive deep into the worlds our conversations would take us, without being disturbed.

We set down on the comfy Muse booths. Soon after, Luke brought the beers and set down with us.

“So how was your guys’ day?” – Luke asked.

“Not bad, not bad. Peter and I were out by Phobos II today. Saw how that little baby is coming along.” – Ron answered. Phobos II was a new child base under construction. It was about one-day’s ride from Phobos I. The site chosen for Phobos II was very special. It was being erected on top of a geothermal hotspot. A facility called The Harvester was also being assembled alongside Phobos II. Its purpose would be the extraction of the free and abundant Geothermal Energy coming off the planet. If it were to be built, it would essentially provide free, readily usable energy for thousands upon thousands of years.

“So how was it coming along?” – asked Alex.

“Well, it has definitely come a long way.” – said Ron as he took sip of his beer.

“The plans for the Harvester should be fully complete soon. It won’t be long before we start building It.” – He added.

“If the Harvester was completed, would we really be fully independent from home” – asked Luke.

“You kidding? We’d have enough energy to last us thousands of years.” – said Alex, confidently.

“He’s right.” – I concurred.

“We could build a whole new world, a better life…” – Ron added.

“That’s amazing.” – Said Luke. Alex looked at me at that point and said with the same hint of hopelessness as before, “Yea, well, we have to build it first.”

“What do you mean?” – Asked Ron.

“Well to build the damn thing we need power, correct?” – he asked. As if he was using the Platonic method of asking questions in order to have the questioner come to the answer.

“Yes…” – Ron answered, Luke nodded and I listened, seeing where Alex was going with this.

“Well, where are we going to find the power?” – Alex continued.

“Umm? The nuclear plants?” – Luke answered naively.

“I don’t see where you’re going with this.” Ron stated.

“Where I’m going is this: the nuclear power plants need Uranium amongst other resources – resources that, up until now, came from back home. But say that we couldn’t have access to the resources we import from back home anymore. That means no Uranium, Lithium, Barium, Hydrogen, etc. How would we run the power plants, how would we run the chemical reactors, drive our vehicles, grow our food?” – said Alex, making a point.

“So what are you sayi-” – Luke began to ask, but Alex interrupted. “What I’m saying” – Alex continued, “is that I heard rumors that we haven’t been able to get into contact with the home base in Earth for the past week. No one has any clue what happened. It’s like they are all gone.”

The look on all three of them reeked of horror. I couldn’t blame them… I was pretty horrified myself.
